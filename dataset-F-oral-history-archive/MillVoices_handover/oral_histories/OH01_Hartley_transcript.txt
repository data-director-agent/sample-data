MILL VOICES ORAL HISTORY PROJECT
Interview OH01
Interviewee: Edna Hartley (b. 1941)
Interviewer: Nadia Pell (NP)
Date: 15 January 2025. Place: interviewee's home, Netherbrook.
Duration: 1 h 14 min. Transcript: full, lightly edited for readability. Checked by NP.

[00:00:12]
NP: Could you start by telling me when you first went into the mill?

EH: I was fifteen. Left school on the Friday and started at Brook Lane Mill on the Monday. My mother had been a weaver there, and her mother before her, so there was never any question. You didn't apply, you just turned up with your mother and she had a word with the tackler.

NP: What was your first job?

EH: Learner on the looms. You'd stand with an older weaver for weeks before they'd let you touch anything. Mine was a woman we called Auntie Bell, though she wasn't anybody's auntie. She'd smack your hand if you reached in without stopping the loom. You were glad of it later, mind. I saw what a shuttle could do.

[00:06:40]
NP: What do you remember about the noise?

EH: Oh, the noise. You can't describe it to anybody who wasn't there. It got into your chest. We all learnt to lip-read, every one of us. You could have a whole conversation across the shed without a sound. My husband used to say I mouthed my words at the dinner table for years after.

NP: Did people talk about their hearing at the time?

EH: Not really. You just got on. Half of us are deaf now, but you'd not have thought to complain. It was a good job, steady, and the money was better than shop work.

[00:15:02]
NP: Tell me about the social side.

EH: That was the best of it. There was the mill trip every summer, a charabanc to the coast, and the Whitsun walk with the chapel. Christmas, the shed would be done up with paper chains and the manager came round with a tin of toffees. We had a netball team for a while. We weren't very good.

NP: Is that the trip in the photograph you lent us?

EH: That's the one, 1962. I'm on the back row, the one with the headscarf. My dad took it with his Brownie. He took all our family photographs.

[00:31:18]
NP: When did things start to change?

EH: You could feel it in the seventies. Orders dropped. They put us on short time, three days a week, and then the rumours started. They brought new looms in at one end and laid people off at the other. When Brook Lane shut in 1979 I went to the sewing factory in town. It wasn't the same. Nobody lip-read there.

[00:52:47]
NP: How do you feel about the mills now?

EH: Proud. People think it was all misery, and some of it was hard, but we made good cloth. Proper cloth. I'd like my grandchildren to hear that from me and not just read it in a book. That's why I said yes to you. Put my name on it. I want them to know it was me.

[END OF EXTRACT TRANSCRIBED]
